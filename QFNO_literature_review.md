# Classical vs. Quantum Fourier Neural Operators for PDE Surrogates: A Literature Foundation

## The operator-learning setting

The problem this project sits in is *operator learning*: rather than fitting a solution to one instance of a partial differential equation (PDE), one learns the solution operator that maps an entire input function (initial condition, coefficient field, forcing) to the PDE solution. [Kovachki et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2108.08481) formalized neural operators as maps between infinite-dimensional function spaces and proved a universal-approximation result for them, which is the theoretical license for the whole family. Two architectures dominate the empirical literature and are the natural baselines here. [Lu et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-021-00302-5) introduced DeepONet, whose branch/trunk decomposition follows directly from an operator universal-approximation theorem. [Li et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2010.08895) introduced the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO), which parameterizes the operator's kernel integral as a multiplication in Fourier space, making each layer a global convolution computed with the FFT. The FNO is the direct object of study for a *quantum* FNO, so its structure matters: a layer lifts the input channel-wise, applies a fast Fourier transform, multiplies the lowest retained modes by learned complex weights, inverts the transform, and adds a pointwise linear residual. The learned spectral multiplication is the component a quantum circuit is meant to replace or augment.

FNOs earned their prominence on hard problems. The original work reported roughly order-of-magnitude error reductions over prior learned solvers on Burgers, Darcy flow, and Navier–Stokes, at inference speeds far below classical solvers ([Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2010.08895)). The same machinery scaled to global weather emulation in FourCastNet ([Pathak et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2202.11214)), which is the strongest existing evidence that spectral operator learning is more than a benchmark curiosity. That track record is also why a quantum variant is worth measuring against a *strong* classical baseline rather than a toy one — the bar is high.

## What the quantum circuit replaces

The motivation for a quantum FNO is that the Fourier transform is exactly where quantum computing has a textbook exponential-depth advantage: the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) acts on the amplitudes of an $n$-qubit state — a $2^n$-dimensional vector — in $O(n^2)$ gates. The idea explored by [Jain et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ad42ce) (the paper this project most directly extends, first posted as [arXiv:2306.15415](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2306.15415)) is to encode the discretized field into a quantum state, use the QFT to move to frequency space, apply a *parameterized* quantum circuit as the learnable spectral filter, invert with the QFT, and read out. They propose both sequential and parallel "quantum Fourier network" layers and demonstrate them on parametric PDEs, framing the appeal as a potential asymptotic reduction in the cost of the spectral mixing step. The most recent entry, a continuous-variable formulation by [Marcandelli et al. (2026, preprint)](https://doi.org/10.20944/preprints202605.1199.v1), pushes the same idea into photonic/qumode hardware rather than qubits, which is worth citing as evidence the design space is still open rather than as a settled baseline.

A parallel and arguably cleaner line of evidence comes from operator learning's other pillar: [Xiao et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2025-06-04-1761) built Quantum DeepONet, showing that the branch/trunk evaluation can be offloaded to quantum circuits with a provable speedup in the inference-time linear algebra. That paper is the better template for *how to make and defend a speedup claim*, because it separates the asymptotic argument from the empirical accuracy check — a discipline the FNO-side literature is still catching up to.

## The claim to be skeptical of

The central tension a rigorous project has to confront is that most "quantum PDE" demonstrations, including quantum FNOs, are run on *classical simulators of small quantum circuits*, so the reported advantage is asymptotic and prospective, not measured. Two hard obstacles sit between the simulation and any real speedup. The first is data loading: encoding a general $2^n$-amplitude field into a quantum state costs $O(2^n)$ gates in the worst case, which can erase the QFT's $O(n^2)$ advantage unless the input has exploitable structure. This caveat the quantum operator-learning papers acknowledge but rarely resolve. The second is trainability: [Larocca et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42254-025-00813-9), the current reference review on barren plateaus, documents how the gradients of deep parameterized quantum circuits vanish exponentially in the qubit count, which is precisely the regime a scaled-up quantum spectral filter would need. Any honest comparison must therefore report gradient-variance and trainability diagnostics as the qubit count grows, not accuracy alone. A quantum FNO that matches the classical one at 6 qubits tells you little about whether it survives at 20.

## Benchmarks and datasets

Using the datasets the field already reports on is what lets a reviewer place your numbers. Three sources cover it. The original FNO datasets — 1D Burgers, 2D Darcy flow, and 2D/3D Navier–Stokes — are the canonical FNO testbed and are publicly distributed by the authors ([Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2010.08895)). For a standardized, versioned, and larger benchmark, [Takamoto et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2210.07182) released PDEBench, which ships downloadable HDF5 datasets (advection, Burgers, diffusion–reaction, 1D/2D/3D compressible and incompressible Navier–Stokes, Darcy) together with baseline FNO/U-Net/autoregressive results and error metrics; it is the recommended primary benchmark here precisely because the baselines are published alongside the data. The quantum-FNO papers themselves ([Jain et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ad42ce); [Xiao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2025-06-04-1761)) test on Burgers and diffusion-type equations, so starting on 1D Burgers and 2D Darcy makes your results directly comparable to both the classical and quantum prior art while staying inside the qubit budget a simulator allows.

## Tooling in Julia

The project's choice of Julia is well supported on both halves. On the classical side, `NeuralOperators.jl` (SciML) provides FNO and DeepONet layers built on Lux, so the baseline is a library call rather than a reimplementation. On the quantum side, [Luo et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2020-10-11-341) built Yao.jl, a differentiable quantum-circuit framework whose defining feature — built-in automatic differentiation of parameterized circuits, including a batched register for statevector simulation — is exactly what a variational quantum spectral layer needs, and it composes with Julia's autodiff so the hybrid model trains end to end. This is the combination that makes a like-for-like classical-versus-quantum FNO feasible on one simulator without leaving a single language.

## A defensible contribution

Taken together, the literature points to a specific, defensible project rather than a race to beat the classical FNO. The classical FNO is a strong, well-benchmarked surrogate; the quantum FNO is a promising but simulator-bound idea whose real advantage is unproven and whose scaling is threatened by data-loading cost and barren plateaus. The contribution with the most staying power is a controlled comparison on shared PDEBench data: match the classical and quantum spectral layers at equal parameter budgets and equal retained-mode counts, then report accuracy *and* the two things the field usually omits — how gradient variance and training stability degrade as qubits increase, and how the encoding cost is accounted for in any speedup claim. That reframes the question from "is quantum better" (not yet answerable on a simulator) to "under what conditions, and with what trainability cost, does a quantum spectral filter match a classical one" — which is answerable, and useful to the people who will run it on real hardware later.
